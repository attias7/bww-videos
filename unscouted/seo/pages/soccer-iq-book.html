What is the Soccer iQ book?

Soccer iQ is a series of two short books by Dan Blank, a college soccer coach for over 20 years who was associate head coach at the University of Georgia. He wrote it after seeing players repeat the same mistakes year after year. Volume 1 was self-published in 2012 and has since been translated into 12 languages, according to his website.

Its reputation is solid. The author's site says Volume 1 was named a Top 5 Book of the Year by the NSCAA Soccer Journal (the coaches' association now called United Soccer Coaches) and has been a #1 best-seller in its Amazon category in the US.

What does Soccer iQ cover?

Soccer iQ covers the small, repeatable decisions that separate a smart player from a careless one: where to stand, when to release the ball, how to deal with second balls and set pieces, and how to avoid the mistakes coaches see every season. Each chapter names one problem and gives a simple fix.

Here's what the format looks like, based on the publisher and reviewer descriptions (we summarise rather than quote):

  • Short chapters. Most run about two pages, so you can read one in five minutes. Across the two volumes there are over 100 tips.
  • Practical topics. Examples mentioned in descriptions and reviews: winning rebounds, playing in the rain, taking quick and accurate throw-ins, and when to be direct as a dribbler.
  • A note for coaches. Many chapters end with a short section on how a coach can train the idea, which a player can use as a practice plan too.
  • Diagrams in Volume 2. Reviewers mention diagrams on many of its roughly 50 tips.
  • A college recruiting chapter. Volume 1 includes a bonus chapter on the recruiting process, useful if you're 15 to 18 and aiming for college soccer.
  • A light tone. Readers often describe it as funny and easy to read, not a tactics textbook.

It's also free of jargon, so a 14-year-old can follow it, which is one reason coaches hand it out to their teams.

Who is Soccer iQ for?

Soccer iQ suits serious youth and high-school players, college-bound players, and the coaches and parents around them. It's most useful if you already play regularly and want to stop making avoidable mistakes. It's less useful if you need deep tactics, position-by-position detail or technical training.

Good fit if you:

  • play in a team every week and keep hearing the same correction from your coach,
  • like short reads you can come back to before games,
  • are heading toward college soccer in the US (the recruiting chapter and the college-coach perspective help),
  • want something cheap to give a teammate or younger sibling.

Less of a fit if you:

  • want detailed tactics for your position (striker, winger, number 10, defender),
  • need technique or fitness work,
  • know the principles already but freeze or choose late in matches. That's a practice problem, not a knowledge problem.

What a book can't train on its own

A book can teach you what a good decision looks like. It can't make you spot the cue and choose in the second you actually have. In a match you get a moving picture, pressure from behind and maybe one look before the ball arrives. Knowing the rule and using it at that speed are two different skills.

This isn't a criticism of Soccer iQ. The coach notes at the end of many chapters exist for this reason: the author expects the ideas to be practised, not just read. The gap is simply that most players read the book, nod along, and never build the practice step. Three things only practice gives you:

  • Time pressure. Reading has no clock. Matches do.
  • Recognition. You have to see the situation in a real picture, from your position, before you can apply the fix.
  • Feedback. You need to know whether your choice was right, quickly, many times.

How to get the most from Soccer iQ: a 4-week routine

The best way to use Soccer iQ is slowly: one chapter at a time, each turned into a rule you test in practice and games. Spread over 4 weeks, three chapters a week gives you 12 habits you've actually tried, instead of 50 you've only read about.

  1. Read one chapter (5 minutes). Don't read ahead.
  2. Write one rule in your own words, under 10 words, in your phone or a notebook. Make it something you can check, like "on a rebound, I attack the ball first".
  3. Turn it into a cue. What do you need to see to use the rule? Write that down too.
  4. Practise it in your next team session or small-sided game. Tell yourself before you start which rule you're testing.
  5. Test it in the game at the weekend. One rule per game, not five.
  6. Review on Sunday: did the situation come up? Did you see it in time? What happened?

Other books by Dan Blank

Dan Blank has written several other soccer books, listed on his website. The ones most relevant to a player aged 15 to 21 are:

  • Shutout Pizza: smarter defending for players and coaches.
  • Possession: teaching a team to keep the ball (written mainly for coaches).
  • High Pressure: winning games by pressing the opponent.
  • Rookie: surviving your freshman year of college soccer.

FAQ

Is the Soccer iQ book worth reading?

For most serious youth players, yes. It's short, cheap and practical, and it rates over 4.2 out of 5 on Goodreads for both volumes. Get more from it by practising each idea in games, not just reading.

How many Soccer iQ books are there?

Two: Soccer iQ: Things That Smart Players Do (2012) and Soccer iQ Vol. 2: More of What Smart Players Do (2014). Dan Blank has also written related books such as Shutout Pizza and Possession.

Which Soccer iQ volume should I read first?

Volume 1. Several Goodreads reviewers of Volume 2 say Volume 1 covers the broader, more useful ideas, so start there if you only read one.

What age is Soccer iQ for?

It's written for players, coaches and parents, and reviewers say it works for middle and high school players as well as college players. The language is simple enough for a young teenager.

Is Soccer iQ available as an audiobook or ebook?

Yes. It's available in paperback, on Kindle and as an audiobook, and Volume 1's ebook is listed on OverDrive, so some libraries lend it free.

Who is Dan Blank?

An American college soccer coach with over 20 years in the college game, including as associate head coach at the University of Georgia. He's written several soccer books, starting with Soccer iQ in 2012.

Can you improve soccer IQ by reading?

Reading helps you understand what good decisions look like. To make them faster in matches you also need practice with time pressure and feedback, such as small-sided games or scenario training like Unscouted's free soccer IQ test.
