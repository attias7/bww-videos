What counts as "professional" in the US?

A professional soccer player is someone paid to play for a club in a professional league. In the US men's game, that means Major League Soccer (MLS), the USL Championship, USL League One or MLS NEXT Pro. Below them sit pre-professional and amateur leagues where players get noticed but mostly aren't paid a salary.

Knowing the levels matters, because "going pro" usually means signing at one of the lower pro levels first, not walking into MLS. In most of the world the sport is called football, and the same idea applies there: most careers start a few levels down.

What are the real odds?

The honest answer is that the odds are small, and no official source gives an exact percentage for men's soccer. The NCAA publishes pro odds for other sports but leaves men's soccer out, saying there are too many professional options (it names MLS and USL) to measure it the same way.

If fewer than 6 in 100 high school players reach college soccer, the pro game is smaller still. So set a goal you can work on ("be the best player in my league, then move up a level") and treat a college place, a USL League Two spot or a semi-pro contract as real wins along the way.

What are the pathways to pro soccer in the US?

There are six main routes: an elite youth club or academy, high school, college, pre-pro summer leagues, open tryouts and combines, and going abroad. Most players who turn pro combine two or three, for example college, then a USL League Two summer, then an open tryout.

What do coaches and scouts look at?

Pro coaches judge four things: technical ability, tactical understanding, physical readiness and professionalism. Brooklyn FC, a USL Championship club, listed exactly those four for its 2026 open tryout. Tactical understanding is mostly decisions: where you move, when you pass and what you see before the ball arrives.

  • Technical: first touch under pressure, passing with both feet, striking the ball cleanly. Not tricks.
  • Tactical: scanning before you receive, choosing the forward option when it's on, and where you stand without the ball.
  • Physical: can you repeat sprints in the 80th minute as well as the 10th?
  • Professionalism: on time, ready, coachable, no sulking after mistakes.

Most players train technique and fitness alone and hope the tactical side appears in games. Our guide to decision-making training shows how to work on it by yourself.

What is a realistic timeline from 15 to 21?

A realistic timeline is: build your base and get into the best team you can at 15–16, get visible to college and pre-pro clubs at 17–18, and test yourself at open tryouts and summer leagues at 18–21. It's later than the academy route but still workable, as long as you keep moving up a level each year.

How to become a professional soccer player: 7 steps

If you're asking "how do I become a professional soccer player" without an academy behind you, these seven steps are the plan. They don't promise a contract, but they put you where pro coaches already look.

  1. Find your true level. Film a full game and rate yourself honestly on scanning, decisions, first touch, off-ball movement, defending and effort. Take the free soccer IQ test as a baseline for decisions.
  2. Get into the highest team you can hold a place in. A regular starter at a higher level is more visible than a star at a lower one.
  3. Train your weakest area every week. One clear target at a time, for example "scan twice before every receive", measured in games.
  4. Study your position. Watch one pro in your role each week, mostly off the ball.
  5. Make yourself easy to judge. A 3 to 5 minute highlight video, one full half, and your details on the first screen.
  6. Choose your route. College recruiting, a summer league, open tryouts or a mix. Check every event on the club's official website.
  7. Review every 12 weeks. Compare your new film with the old. If nothing changed, change the plan before you change the video.

What can you do each week without an academy?

Without an academy, you build your own week: team training and games stay the base, then you add two short solo technical sessions, one decision-making session, one match-analysis session and proper recovery. About 3 to 4 extra hours a week is enough if every session has a clear target.

FAQ

How do I become a professional soccer player if I'm not in an academy?

Play at the highest level you can hold a place in, train your weaknesses every week, and use the late routes: college soccer, summer leagues like USL League Two, and open tryouts that USL and MLS NEXT Pro clubs advertise on their own websites. Nobody can guarantee a contract.

Is 18 or 21 too late to become a professional soccer player?

It's late compared with the academy route, but not closed. The pro and pre-pro open tryouts we checked set minimum ages between 16 and 18, and one MLS NEXT Pro tryout we checked was for ages 18 to 25. The later you start, the more you need to stand out at your current level first.

What percentage of soccer players go pro?

No official figure exists for US men's soccer. The NCAA leaves soccer out of its pro odds because of the many pro options, but its 2024-25 data shows only 5.9% of high school boys' soccer players reach NCAA soccer and 1.4% reach Division I.

Do you have to go to college to become a pro soccer player?

No. Some players sign from academies, MLS NEXT Pro teams or open tryouts. But college is the biggest late route in the US, and it gives you a degree if soccer doesn't work out.

How much do pro soccer tryouts cost?

On the official club pages we checked in October 2026, fees ranged from $40 for a USL League Two tryout to $225 for an MLS NEXT Pro tryout. Always register through the club's own website.

Can I go to Europe to become a professional soccer player?

FIFA rules only allow international transfers of players over 18, with narrow exceptions such as your parents moving abroad for non-football reasons. Be very careful with paid "European trial" offers from agents.

What skills do you need to be a professional soccer player?

Clubs judge technical ability, tactical understanding, physical readiness and professionalism. Good decisions under pressure, like scanning and choosing the forward pass, often separate players who look similar in drills.
