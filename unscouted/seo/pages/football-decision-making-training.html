What is decision making in football?

Decision making in football is choosing what to do with the ball, or without it, in the second or two you have: pass, dribble, shoot, hold, press, cover or run. A good decision is the option that helps your team most in that exact picture, done at the right moment and done fully, not halfway.

The FA's Boot Room article on developing decision-makers describes it in a similar way: picking the technique, action or tactic that benefits the team. It also makes the point that at higher levels, where players are similarly fit and skilful, reading the game is a big part of what separates them.

Every decision has two parts:

  • Seeing the picture: where the space is, who is free, who is closing you, how deep their back line is.
  • Choosing from it: knowing which option suits that picture, and doing it without hesitating.

Both can be trained. Neither needs an academy, a coach or special equipment.

Why do you make the wrong decision?

You usually make the wrong decision because you saw the picture too late, not because you picked badly from a full picture. If you only watch the ball rolling towards you, you find out about the defender behind you when he takes it. You chose the only option you knew about.

Think about the last time you lost the ball in midfield. Did you know a defender was behind you before the ball arrived? The mistake happened seconds earlier, when you stopped looking around.

What do good decision-makers do differently?

Good decision-makers see the picture earlier and recognise it faster. They look around before the ball comes, they predict what will happen next instead of reacting to it, and they have seen so many similar moments that the choice is often half made before their first touch. None of that is a gift you either have or don't.

The FA Boot Room article lists, from research on expert players, skills like anticipation, recognising patterns of play, picking out the important information and tactical knowledge. Here is what that means on the pitch:

The 3-step decision: look, compare, commit

The simplest way to make better decisions in football is to run the same three steps every time the ball is coming to you: look around before it arrives, compare your three main options, then commit fully to one. It works in every position and you can practise each step on its own.

1. Look before the ball arrives

Check over your shoulder while the pass is being played and while it travels. You are looking for three things: the nearest opponent, the nearest free teammate, and the space behind their line. If you only manage one look, make it the one just before the ball reaches you. Open your body to the pitch (half-turned, not square to the passer) so the picture stays in view.

2. Compare three options

Nearly every moment in football comes down to about three choices:

  • Forward: pass, run or shoot towards goal.
  • Keep it: a safe pass back or sideways, or shielding the ball.
  • Change it: switch the play, go 1v1, or play a one-two.

You don't need to think through all three every time. Just know they exist, so you aren't stuck with the first idea you have. A useful order of questions: can I go forward? If not, can I keep it and move it on? If not, can I change the side or beat my man?

3. Commit

A good option done halfway is usually worse than an average option done fully. Once you choose, do it properly. Hesitation is where a lot of lost balls come from.

Honest caveat: there is rarely one "correct" answer in football. The situations below have a best option for the picture shown, the one a good coach would want. Change one player's position and the answer can change. That is the whole point: the answer comes from the picture, not from a rule.

Situation 1: should the winger take on the full-back?

You are the right winger. It is 0-0 after 34 minutes. Your right back plays it up the line to you. Their left back is in front of you. Look at the board for a few seconds before reading the options.

What do you do?

  • A Play it inside to your 10.
  • B Lay it back to your right back and go again.
  • C Take him on down the line. (pro pick)

Why C: Look behind their left back. Their nearest centre back is about 17 metres inside him, and their winger has stayed up near halfway. Nobody is covering him. He has also set himself on your inside to stop you cutting in. That leaves the line. Beat him there and you are at the byline with a cross to make, which is exactly the situation a winger wants.

Why not A: Your 10 looks free, but their defensive midfielder is standing in the passing lane. If it gets cut out, they break down your side, where your right back has just come from.

Why not B: It is safe and keeps the ball. If their full-back had a covering centre back close by, or their winger had tracked back to double you, laying it back would be the smart choice. Here you would be giving up the best 1v1 you will get all half.

The lesson: before you decide whether to take a full-back on, look at who is behind him, not just at him. More on the role in our winger guide, and winger-specific situations like this are in the Winger pack.

Situation 2: should the number 10 turn?

You are the number 10. It is 0-0 in the 58th minute. Your midfielder plays it into your feet between their lines. Your back is to their goal.

What do you do?

  • A Turn and run at their back line.
  • B Lay it off first time to your midfielder running up. (pro pick)
  • C Shield it and wait for support.

Why B: One of their centre backs has followed you out and is about 4 metres from your back. That means two things. You can't turn safely, and there is now a gap in their back line where he used to be. Your striker is standing alone in it. You can't reach him from where you are, but your other midfielder is arriving facing forward. Set it to him first time and he can play it into that gap.

Why not A: Turning is usually what a 10 wants to do between the lines, and if nobody had followed you it would be the right call. With a centre back that close, he takes the ball off you while your team is pushed forward.

Why not C: Shielding keeps the ball for a second or two. That is useful when no help is coming. Here help is already arriving, and every second you hold it lets their midfielders squeeze you from behind.

If you didn't check your shoulder, you wouldn't know the centre back was there until he was on you. See our number 10 guide for the role, and more situations are in the Number 10 pack.

Situation 3: should the centre back close down a 2v1?

You are a centre back. It is 1-1 after 71 minutes. Your corner was cleared and they are breaking. Two of them are coming at you. Your goal is at the top of this board.

What do you do?

  • A Go and close the ball carrier now.
  • B Drop off between them and slow it down. (pro pick)
  • C Run across with the free runner.

Why B: Look at your midfielder: he is sprinting back and is only a few seconds away. The ball is still nearly 40 metres from your goal, so you have room to give ground. Back off slowly, stay between the ball and the other runner, and make the carrier decide. If he passes, you are close enough to step across. If he keeps going, you are still in front of him. Every second you buy turns a 2v1 into a 2v2.

Why not A: It feels brave, and a lot of players would pick it. But nobody is behind you. The moment you commit, he slips it to the other runner and he is through on your keeper.

Why not C: It cuts out the pass, but gives the ball carrier a straight run at goal. Go with the runner only once the carrier has clearly decided to go alone.

Here the safe-looking option is right, while in Situation 1 the bold one was. That is why rules like "always be positive" don't work. Our centre back guide covers more of these, and defending situations like this are in the Defender pack.

What decisions does each position face?

Each position faces a few decisions again and again. A winger mostly decides when to take the full-back on, a number 10 when to turn, a striker when to run in behind, and a centre back when to step in or drop. Learn the three or four repeat decisions of your position first.

How to train decisions on your own

You can't practise a 2v1 alone in the park, but you can practise reading it. Training decisions alone means three things: working through match situations and deciding before you see the answer, watching games with one question and pausing to predict, and reviewing your own decisions after every match. Each takes 5 to 15 minutes.

1. Work through match situations

Pictures like the three above train the "see it, compare, choose" part without any pressure. Do a few at a time. Before you look at the options, say out loud what you would do and which detail made you choose it. If you got it wrong, find the detail you missed. That is the part that carries into games.

There is some research support for this kind of training. A 2022 systematic review in Frontiers in Human Neuroscience (Zhao and colleagues) looked at 10 studies of video-based training in football players. In 8 of them, players who did the video training improved on anticipation or decision-making tests from before to after. The authors also note that some results were inconsistent because the studies used different durations and set-ups, and that more research is needed on whether the gains last and carry over to matches. Treat it as "promising", not "proven".

2. Watch games with one question

Watching a full match "to learn" usually turns into watching it as a fan. Pick one player in your position and one question for 15 minutes, for example: "When does the winger take his full-back on, and when does he lay it back?" Pause before he receives the ball, guess what he will do, then play it. Our guide to analysing a football match as a player walks through this step by step.

3. Review your own decisions after each match

Straight after a game, write down three moments: one good decision, one bad one, and one you aren't sure about. Use the same four questions each time:

QuestionExample answer
What was the moment?Received in midfield, 60th minute, lost it
What did I see before the ball came?Only the passer
What did I miss?Their 8 closing from behind, free full-back on my left
What will I do next time?Check my left shoulder as the pass is played

If you have video, check it. After a few weeks you will see the same mistake keep coming up. That one mistake is your training plan.

How to train decisions with a few friends

With two to six friends, small-sided games with a simple rule change are the best decision training you can set up. Coaches call these constraints. Instead of being told what to do, the rule makes the good decision the one that pays, so you learn to spot it yourself in a game-like picture.

The FA Boot Room article recommends varied, game-like practice over the same drill repeated, and Football DNA's coaching article builds sessions on exactly this idea. Here are rule changes that work with small numbers:

GameRuleDecision it trains
2v1 to a small goalAttackers have 6 seconds to scorePass or go alone, and when to release
3v1 rondoTwo touches max; a pass between the two closest players does not countLook before receiving, play away from pressure
3v3 short, wide pitchA goal after switching sides counts doubleWhen to switch play
2v2 plus a neutralThe neutral plays for whoever has the ball, max 1 touchUsing the free man, moving to help
Any small gameA goal within 10 seconds of winning the ball counts doubleGo forward fast after a turnover
Any small gameBefore receiving, the passer shouts "turn" or "man on" only sometimesTrust your own look, not the call

Keep score and swap teams. The pressure of a real opponent is the part you can't get from a screen.

How do you stop being scared of mistakes?

You stop being scared of mistakes by judging your decisions, not just the result. If you saw the picture, chose the right option and the pass was slightly off, that was a good decision with a technique error. If you never looked, that was a decision error even if it worked. Separating the two lets you take the right risk.

Players who fear mistakes tend to pick the safe sideways pass every time. Sometimes that is right (see Situation 3). But if it is your only answer, defenders learn it fast. A few habits help:

  • Have a plan before the ball comes. Most panic comes from not knowing what you will do. One look and one plan removes a lot of it.
  • Grade yourself on the look and the choice. In your match review, mark each moment "good decision / good execution" separately.
  • Reset after a mistake. Pick one short cue, like a breath and "next ball", and use it every time. The next decision matters more than the last one.
  • Take risks where they pay. In the final third, a forward option that fails 2 times in 3 can still be the right choice. In your own box, it usually isn't.

A 15-minute routine and a weekly plan

A 15-minute daily routine is enough to build the habit: 5 minutes of match situations, 5 minutes of watching one player and predicting, and 5 minutes of wall passes with a scan before each touch. Add one small-sided game and one match review each week. It fits around school, work or your normal team training.

How do you know you are improving?

You know your decision making is improving when you are surprised less often, you lose the ball under pressure less, and your match reviews show the same mistake fading. Track three simple numbers for four weeks: situations answered right, scans before receiving in a short clip of your game, and bad decisions per match review.

  • Situation score: out of every 10 situations, how many did you get right before reading the answer?
  • Scan count: if you have video, count how often you look before receiving in 10 touches. Our free scanning test also shows how much of the picture you take in from a quick look.
  • Review tally: how many of your three review moments were "didn't look"? That number should drop.

FAQ

How can I improve my decision making in football?

Look around before the ball arrives, compare three options (forward, keep it, change it) and commit to one. Train it with match situations, small-sided games with rules that force choices, and a short review of your own decisions after each game.

Why do I make bad decisions in football?

Usually because you saw the picture too late. If you only look at the ball while it is coming to you, you only find out about the defender behind you or the free teammate when it is too late to use them. Look around before the ball arrives and you will have more options to choose from.

How do pro players decide so fast?

They have usually seen the picture before the ball reaches them, so the decision is already half made. They have also seen thousands of similar situations, so they recognise patterns quickly instead of working everything out from scratch.

How can I make quicker decisions on the ball?

Look over your shoulder while the pass is travelling, decide your first option before the ball arrives, and keep a second option in mind in case it closes. Training with match situations helps you recognise common pictures faster.

Can you train decision making in football alone?

You can train the reading part alone: match situations, watching games and pausing to predict, reviewing your own decisions, and wall drills with a scan. Small-sided games with friends then test it under real pressure.

Does watching football improve decision making?

It can, if you watch with a purpose. Follow one player in your position, pause before he receives the ball, and predict what he will do. Watching casually as a fan teaches much less.

What drills improve decision making in football?

Small-sided games with a rule change work best, such as a 2v1 with a 6-second limit, a two-touch rondo, or a game where a goal after a switch counts double. Alone, use wall passes where you look over your shoulder and choose a direction before each touch.

What is scanning in football?

Scanning means quick looks around you, mostly over your shoulders, before the ball reaches you. It tells you where opponents, teammates and space are, so you can decide what to do before your first touch.
