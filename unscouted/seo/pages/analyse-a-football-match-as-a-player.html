What's the difference between watching as a fan and analysing as a player?

A fan follows the ball and reacts to goals, shots and fouls. A player analysing a match follows one person in their own position, mostly when that person doesn't have the ball, and writes down what they do so it can be copied. The first is entertainment. The second is homework for your next game.

Most guides on how to analyse (or analyze, if you call it soccer) a match are written for analysts, coaches or bettors: formations, pressing triggers, expected goals. As a player, you're studying one job: yours. A centre-back watching a centre-back, a winger watching a winger. Most of what that player does happens without the ball, the part a TV camera often cuts away from.

The rule: for the whole time you're analysing, the ball is not your subject. If you catch yourself watching the ball, find your player again.

How do you analyse a football match step by step?

Analyse a match in six steps: pick one player and one question, set up a replay with notes ready, read the team's shape in the first ten minutes, track five things on your player, pause before key moments to predict their choice, then compare with your own match and set one countable target. One half is enough to start.

What should you do before kick-off?

Before kick-off, make three choices: which player you'll follow, which single question you want answered, and how you'll watch. Pick someone in your role, in a system close to your team's. Write a question about something you struggle with. Then set up a full replay with commentary off and your worksheet ready.

1. Pick one player in your position

Choose someone who plays your role in a system close to your team's. If you're a full-back in a back four, watch a full-back in a back four, not a wing-back in a back five. Check the line-ups before the game so you know the formation and whether your player is starting in their usual role. If you're not sure who to pick, pick the player whose job you'd most like to copy.

2. Pick one question

Write down one question you want answered. Good questions are specific to something you struggle with. For example:

  • "Where does a holding midfielder stand when the centre-backs have the ball?"
  • "What does a winger do when the full-back behind them has the ball?"
  • "How does a striker get free from a centre-back who's marking tight?"

One question stops you trying to see everything and seeing nothing. Decide what you'll ignore too: if your question is about receiving, ignore set pieces this time.

3. Set up how you watch

  • Full replays beat highlights. Normal highlights only show the ball. If you only have a TV broadcast, accept that your player will sometimes be off-screen and note what you can.
  • Commentary off. Commentators talk about the ball and the story of the game. You want your own eyes on your player.
  • Pause and rewind are allowed. You're not watching live for fun. Rewinding a moment twice is how you learn it.
  • Worksheet ready (paper or a notes app), with the five rows already written in.

How do you read the phases of play around your player?

Split the match into five phases: your team with the ball, your team without it, the moment you lose it, the moment you win it, and set pieces. Your player has a different job in each. In the first ten minutes, note where they stand in each phase, so you know what "normal" looks like before you start counting.

Analysts also split the pitch into thirds (defensive, middle, attacking). You don't need a full tactical report. You just need to know where your player's job happens.

What are the 5 things to track on one player?

Track five things that a player can copy: scans before receiving, body shape when receiving, first-touch direction, off-ball runs, and what they do in the 3 seconds after their team loses the ball. All five are decisions, not physical gifts, so you can change them in your own game this week.

You don't need to track all five every time. Start with two, add more as it gets easier. Use tally marks: one line each time you see it.

1. Scans before receiving

Does your player look over their shoulder before the ball reaches them? Count how many times they turn their head in the few seconds before a pass arrives. There is research behind this one. A 2020 study of one Premier League club's players across a season (Jordet and colleagues, in Frontiers in Psychology) found that players who scanned more before receiving were slightly more likely to complete their pass. The authors say the effect was small and the study shows a link, not proof of cause, but it's a habit you can see and copy. Our guide on how to scan shows how to train it.

2. Body shape when receiving

Is their body open, side-on to the pitch, so they can see forward and play forward? Or square to the passer with their back to where they want to go? Mark "open" or "closed" for each receive you can see.

3. First-touch direction

Where does the first touch take the ball: forward, sideways, backwards, or dead at their feet? A first touch that moves the ball away from pressure and towards the next action is often a decision made before the ball arrived.

4. Off-ball runs

When a teammate has the ball, what does your player do? Count runs and note the type: towards the ball to offer a pass, in behind, or a run that drags a defender away to free someone else. Also note when they stand still.

5. The 3 seconds after losing the ball

The moment their team loses possession, does your player press the ball, cover a space, or switch off? This is where effort and understanding show, and it's rarely in highlights.

How do you take notes without missing the game?

Take notes with tallies, short forms and minute stamps, never full sentences. Draw two columns, in possession and out of possession, and add a mark each time you see one of your five things. Write a three-word note only for moments worth rewinding, with the minute, then write properly at half-time.

If you write sentences live, you'll look down and miss the next action. Use short forms you'll understand later. Here's a starter set. Change it to suit you.

At half-time, spend five minutes turning your marks into two or three plain sentences. That's your answer to the question, or the start of it.

What does a worked match analysis look like?

Here's a worked example: a central midfielder follows the deeper midfielder in one team for a half, with one question about getting free from a marker. The tallies point to a single clear habit, short late movement after a shoulder check, that the player can then test in their own next match.

The notes below are made up to show the method. They're not from a real match and the numbers aren't real stats. Your own notes will look different.

Setup. You play central midfield in a 4-3-3. You watch the first half of a match, following only the deeper central midfielder on one team. Your question: "How does he get free to receive when the opponent's number 10 is close to him?"

Answer to the question: he gets free with short, late movements away from his marker, after checking where that marker is. He doesn't sprint. He times it.

Pause and predict: try it yourself

Stop the video just before your player gets the ball, decide what you'd do, then press play and compare. Here it is as a tactics board: you're the holding midfielder, your right centre-back wants to pass into you, and their 10 is close.

  • A Stay where you are and call for it
  • B Drop deep between your centre-backs
  • C Slide a few steps right, away from their 10 (pro pick)

Why C: their 10 is close on your left, and their striker stands between your centre-backs, so dropping walks you into him. A few late steps right open a clean lane. Do 3–5 of these pauses per half and you'll start seeing these cues live.

Which football stats matter when you analyse a match as a player?

Team stats like expected goals (xG), PPDA and pass accuracy describe the whole match and are useful for understanding why a team won. For your own development, they matter less than simple counts you take yourself on one player: scans, open receives, forward touches, runs and reactions after losing the ball. Use team stats for context, your counts for training.

StatWhat it measuresUseful to you as a player?
Expected goals (xG)How likely a shot was to be scored, based on similar past shots (position, angle, type of chance)Some, for strikers: shows whether you get into good shooting spots
Pass accuracyShare of passes that reached a teammateA little. Safe sideways passes push it up, so read it with care
PPDA (passes per defensive action)How many passes a team allows the opponent before trying to win the ball. Lower means more pressingContext only: tells you how hard a team presses, not what one player did
Shot-creating actionsThe actions (passes, dribbles, fouls won) that lead directly to a shotSome, for attackers and 10s
Your own counts (scans, body shape, touch direction, runs, 3-second reaction)Decisions one player makes again and againYes. These are the habits you can copy and train this week

Honest note: different data providers calculate xG and PPDA in slightly different ways, so numbers from two sites for the same match won't always match.

How do you analyse your own football match?

To analyse your own match, get video if you can, find every moment you were involved, and fill in the same five rows you use for pros. Write evidence, not feelings: "34', received closed, turned into pressure". Then find your biggest gap compared with the pro you studied and turn it into one target.

Getting footage

  • Ask first. Many clubs already film games. Ask your coach if you can get the video.
  • Filming yourself: a parent or friend with a phone on a stand, as high as possible near the halfway line, filming wide enough to show players around you, not just the ball.
  • No video? Write down what you remember straight after the game, honestly, and ask a teammate or coach for one thing they saw.

Reviewing it

  1. Watch once at normal speed to remember the flow.
  2. Watch again and write the minute of every moment you're involved, with and without the ball.
  3. For each moment, mark the five things: did you scan, open or closed, touch direction, run type, reaction after losing it.
  4. Pick the two clips that hurt most and two that went best. What was different before the ball arrived?

Don't compare speed or strength with the pro. Compare what you can change by deciding to: where you look, how you stand, which way you take your touch, when you move.

What should you track for your position?

Track the same five habits in every position, then add two or three position jobs. A striker adds movement against the centre-back and shot positions. A winger adds where they receive before a 1v1. A centre-back adds body shape on the ball and line control. A midfielder adds where they stand in build-up.

How do you turn match analysis into training?

Turn your analysis into training by choosing one gap and writing it as a target you can count, for example "check both shoulders before every pass I receive", then practise it all week and check it in your next match. One target per week beats five vague ones, because you can tell whether it worked.

  • Weak: "Scan more."
  • Better: "Check both shoulders before every pass I receive in training this week."
  • Best: "Check both shoulders before every pass I receive. A teammate counts how many I miss in the first 10 minutes of the session."

Then use it in the next match and mark it on the worksheet. Next week, watch the same player again or pick a new question. After a few weeks you'll have a list of habits copied from players in your position, each tested in your own games. Which games should you watch first?

Start with games where your player's team has a clear style, because patterns are easier to spot. Choose a match where your player is heavily involved, in a system close to your team's, with a full replay available. A one-sided game where your player barely touches the ball teaches you little.

A few starting points by position, with what to look for. These are things to check for yourself, not claims about any one match:

  • Holding midfielder: a deep midfielder in a possession team, such as Rodri. Watch where he stands when his centre-backs have the ball, and how often he looks around before it arrives.
  • Striker: a striker who links play as well as scores, such as Harry Kane. Watch when he drops towards the ball and when he stays high, and what happens to the space he leaves.
  • Winger: a winger who beats players one-on-one. Watch the moments before the 1v1: where they receive, which foot, and which way their body faces.
  • Centre-back: a centre-back in a team that plays out from the back. Watch body shape when receiving from the goalkeeper and where the first pass goes.

What are the most common mistakes when analysing a match?

The most common mistakes are watching the ball instead of your player, trying to track everything at once, writing so much you miss play, studying a player in a very different role, and finishing with notes but no target. Each one has a simple fix, shown in the table below.

MistakeFix
Following the ballSay your player's name in your head every time the ball moves
Tracking everythingTwo of the five things, one question
Writing sentences liveTallies and short forms, sentences at half-time
Wrong role modelSame position, similar system to your team
Copying physical stuffCopy decisions: looks, body shape, touch, timing
Notes with no actionOne countable target, checked in your next match

FAQ

How do footballers analyse games?

Players usually focus on their own role rather than the whole game. Pick one player in your position, track a few specific things like scanning, body shape and off-ball runs, then compare with your own match and choose one thing to work on.

What should I look for when watching football to improve?

Watch what one player in your position does without the ball: scans before receiving, body shape, where the first touch goes, the runs they make, and what they do in the 3 seconds after their team loses the ball.

How can I analyse my own football performance?

Film a match if you can, or ask a teammate or coach to watch. Rate yourself on the same five things you track for pros, write down evidence for each, find your biggest gap and turn it into one countable training target for the week.

Which players should I watch for my position?

Pick a player who plays your role in a system close to your team's. A full-back in a back four should watch a full-back in a back four. Teams with a clear style make patterns easier to spot.

Does watching football make you a better player?

Watching as a fan helps a little. Watching with a plan helps more: one player, one question, notes, then testing what you learned in your own training and matches.

Should I watch full matches or highlights?

Full matches or extended highlights. Normal highlights follow the ball, so you miss what your player does without it, which is a big part of their job.

How long does it take to analyse a football match?

About 80 minutes for one half with the six-step method, including comparing with your own game. Start with one half a week and build up to a full match once the note system feels easy.

Should I turn off the commentary when analysing a match?

Yes. Commentators talk about the ball and the story of the game, which pulls your eyes away from your player. Turn it off and keep your attention on the one player you chose.
