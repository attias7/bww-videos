What is scanning in soccer?

Scanning is a quick turn of the head that takes your eyes off the ball for a moment so you can see what is around and behind you. You do it before you receive, so that when the ball arrives you already know where the pressure is, who is free and where the space is.

Coaches also call it "checking your shoulder" or "taking a picture". In football (the name for soccer outside the US), researcher Geir Jordet and colleagues define a scan as "an active head movement where the face (and hence, the eyes) is temporarily directed away from the ball". Note the two parts: your head turns, not just your eyes, and it is temporary. You look away, take the picture, and look back.

Why does scanning matter? What the research says

Scanning matters because it lets you know where pressure, teammates and space are before your first touch, so you can decide earlier and keep the ball more. Research on Premier League players found that players who scanned more were slightly more likely to complete their next pass. It helps, but it is not the only thing that matters.

The best-known study followed 27 players from one Premier League club across 21 home matches in 2017/18, covering 9,574 possessions. Players who scanned more often before receiving had a slightly higher chance of completing their pass. The authors describe scanning as having "a small, but positive role" in performance.

Other findings from the same paper (Jordet et al., 2020, Frontiers in Psychology):

  • Players averaged about 0.44 scans per second in the seconds before they received the ball.
  • They scanned more before passing than before dribbling or shooting.
  • Central midfielders and central defenders scanned most, forwards least.
  • Scanning dropped under high pressure and near the touchlines.

Keep it in proportion. It was one club, home games only, with scans counted by hand from video, and the authors write that they "do not believe or claim that scanning is the conclusive variable" in performance. Scanning is worth training, but it is one skill among many, not a magic fix.

How often do pro players scan?

Top central midfielders scan very often. In Geir Jordet's earlier research, reported by Sky Sports, Xavi averaged 0.83 scans per second before receiving, Frank Lampard 0.62 and Steven Gerrard 0.61, and Jordet called anything over 0.50 "very high". The Premier League study above found an average of about 0.44 across all positions.

That earlier work looked at 64 players over 118 games, covering 1,279 game situations, according to the same Sky Sports report. In simple terms:

When should you scan? The two moments that matter most

Scan just before a teammate plays the ball to you, and again while the ball is travelling towards you. Then look back at the ball before it arrives so you can control it. Those two looks give you the freshest picture without costing you your first touch.

What should you look for when you scan?

Look for three things: the nearest opponent and which side he is coming from, the nearest free teammate, and the space you could turn or play into. If you only get one look, use it to find the pressure, because that tells you which way you can't go.

A scan that answers none of these is just a head turn, which is why the drills below make you name what you saw.

What should each position scan for?

Each position scans for slightly different things. Midfielders mainly look for pressure behind them and the free player, defenders look for runners behind them and spare passing options, and wingers and forwards look at the last defender and the space in behind. Central midfielders and central defenders scanned most in the Premier League study, forwards least.

Is peripheral vision enough, or do you have to turn your head?

Peripheral vision helps, but it does not replace scanning. Out of the corner of your eye you can see movement to the sides, but not what is behind you, and that is where most pressure on a receiving midfielder comes from. That is why the research definition of a scan is a head movement, not just an eye movement.

Use both. Keep your head up when you dribble so your side vision picks up movement, and turn your head when you need the picture behind you. Drill 2 below trains the first; drills 1, 3 and 4 train the second.

Two situations where one look decides it

The best way to see why scanning matters is to look at moments where the right answer depends on something behind you. In both situations below, the player who checks a shoulder makes a different, better choice than the player who only watches the ball. Look at each board before reading the options.

Situation 1: central midfielder receiving from your center back

It is 0-0 after 22 minutes. Your right center back passes it into you in your own half. You are facing your own goal.

What do you do?

  • A Turn to your left and drive forward.
  • B Open up and turn to your right. (pro pick)
  • C Play it back first time to your left center back.

Why B: A look over your left shoulder shows their midfielder closing from that side. A look over your right shows their other midfielder more than 20 metres away and empty space. Open your body as the ball arrives and turn right.

Why not A: Fine when nobody is there. Here you turn straight into the player closing you.

Why not C: Safe, and right if both sides were closed. Here you give away space that one look would have shown you.

Situation 2: center back defending a cross

It is 1-0 to you after 80 minutes. Their right winger is about to cross from near your byline. Your center back partner is marking their striker. Your goal is at the top.

What do you do?

  • A Stay level with your partner and watch the ball.
  • B Drop and shift towards the back post. (pro pick)
  • C Step to the near post to attack the cross.

Why B: Check your shoulder and you see their left winger free at the back post, with your right back still about 20 metres away. Your partner has the striker, so the danger is behind you. Shift across so you can see the ball and the runner at the same time.

Why not A: You won't know the runner is there until he heads it.

Why not C: Good when the back post is covered. Here a cross over your head is a free header.

Why do you forget to scan, and how do you make it a habit?

You forget to scan because in a match your attention goes to the ball and nothing reminds you to look away. Habits stick when they are tied to a trigger. Link the scan to something that happens every time, like a teammate's head going down to pass, so the look becomes automatic.

TriggerYour scan
A teammate gets the ballFirst look: where am I free?
His head goes down to pass to youSecond look, the side you can't see
The ball goes out of playBig look around: their shape, your runners
The ball switches to the other sideDefenders: check behind for runners
You win the ball backLook forward first: is there a quick counter?

Three more things help it stick:

  • Train it every session, not once. A few minutes of scanning in every warm-up beats one long session a month.
  • Make it part of your first touch. If every look decides the direction of your touch, you will start to miss it when you don't look.
  • Give yourself one match goal. "Look before every touch in the first 15 minutes" is easier to keep than "scan more".

7 scanning drills you can do alone, with a friend or a small group

These drills need a wall, a ball, a few cones and sometimes one friend or a small group. Each one makes you look and then say or use what you saw. Pick 2 or 3 per session, 15–20 minutes in total, a few times a week, with short rests so your touch stays sharp.

1. Wall pass with a shoulder check (alone)

  1. Stand 5–8 metres from a wall with a different-coloured cone 3 metres behind you on each side.
  2. Pass against the wall. While the ball comes back, look over one shoulder and say the cone's colour.
  3. Eyes back, control, pass again. Alternate shoulders.
  4. Progression: take your first touch towards the side you just checked.

2. Beep-and-name dribble (alone)

  1. Mark a 10 x 10 metre square. Set a phone interval timer to beep every 4 seconds.
  2. Dribble inside the square. On each beep, look over one shoulder and name one thing you see.
  3. 60 seconds on, 30 off, 4 times. Progression: beep every 3 seconds.

Some apps flash random colours or numbers for this, for example ProScan360 ($0.99 one-off on the App Store, checked October 2026). A plain timer works fine.

3. Call the fingers (with a friend)

  1. Stand 6 metres from a wall. Your friend stands 4–5 metres behind you.
  2. Pass against the wall. Once the ball has left your foot, your friend holds up 1 to 5 fingers on either side of you.
  3. Turn your head, shout the number before your first touch.
  4. Progression: odd number, first touch left; even, right. Swap after 15.

4. Turn away from pressure (with a friend)

  1. Face a wall from 6 metres. Your friend stands 3 metres behind you, slightly left or right.
  2. Pass against the wall and check both shoulders while it comes back.
  3. Your friend steps in as you receive. Turn away from his side and dribble past a cone 5 metres away.
  4. He changes side at random each rep. Swap after 10.

5. Gate pass (with a friend)

  1. Set two small gates (cones 1 metre apart) 8 metres behind you, one left, one right.
  2. Your friend passes to you from the front, then sprints to stand in one gate.
  3. Scan while the ball travels, then turn and pass through the other gate. Swap after 10.

6. Signal rondo (5 to 7 players)

  1. Mark an 8 x 8 metre square. Play 3v1 or 4v2 keep-ball.
  2. One player stands outside the square holding a bib or cone. Raised means two touches max, down means one touch.
  3. The signal is behind some players, so they have to look before receiving to know how many touches they have.
  4. Two minutes per round, change the signal holder each round. Progression: a second signal that means "switch to the far player".

7. Count your scans in team training

In one 10-minute block of team training, like a rondo, use one rule: look over a shoulder before every touch. Afterwards, score yourself honestly: most touches, some, or hardly any. If someone films a few minutes, count your looks and compare week to week.

Keep it sensible: these drills are light, but your legs and focus still get tired. Stop if anything hurts and keep your rest days. Short regular sessions build the habit better than one long one.

A 4-week scanning plan

Train scanning for four weeks with three short sessions a week, moving from simple looks to looks that decide your touch, then to looks under pressure. Each session takes 15–20 minutes and fits into a warm-up. Keep the same match goal all month: one look before every touch.

How can you practise scanning while watching matches?

Follow one player in your position and count how often he turns his head in the few seconds before he receives. Then pause just before he receives and guess what he will do. This shows you when good players look and what they do with what they see.

  1. Pick one player. Central midfielders are easiest to learn from, since they scan the most.
  2. Use a wide angle. TV follows the ball and cuts off a lot of scans. Tactical-camera replays or watching from the stands show more.
  3. Pause and predict before he receives: what did his look show him?
  4. Write one line after 15 minutes, like "he always checks his left shoulder before a pass from the left back".

What are the most common scanning mistakes?

The most common mistake is looking without seeing: turning your head because a coach said so, without taking in where the pressure or the free player is. The others are looking too late, always checking the same side, and stopping to look.

  • Looking without seeing. Fix: name something after each look in drills.
  • Looking as the ball arrives. You miss your touch. Look while it travels instead.
  • One early look only. The picture changes in seconds. Take an early and a late look.
  • Always the same shoulder. Alternate on purpose in drills.
  • Standing still to scan. Look while you move into space.
  • Seeing it but not using it. Practise decisions from match situations so you know what each picture means.

That last step is decision making. Our guide to decision making in football covers what comes after the look.

How do you know your scanning is improving?

You know your scanning is improving when you get caught on the ball less, turn away from pressure more often, and can say where the free player was after a quick look. The simplest checks are a scan count from video of your games and a repeatable test you can take every few weeks.

  • Scan count: from a few minutes of video, count how many of 10 receptions you looked before. Compare month to month.
  • Picture test: Unscouted's free football scanning test shows a board for 2 seconds, hides it, then asks what you saw. 10 rounds, score out of 10.
  • Match notes: after each game, write how many times you were surprised by a defender. That number should drop.

FAQ

What is scanning in soccer?

Scanning is a quick turn of the head, away from the ball, to see where opponents, teammates and space are before you receive. It helps you know what to do before your first touch.

How often should you scan in soccer?

There is no fixed number. In one study of Premier League players, the average was about 0.44 scans per second before receiving, a little under one look every two seconds. A good target is at least one look before the pass to you and one while it travels.

Can you practise scanning alone?

Yes. Wall passes with a shoulder check and dribbling with a timer that tells you when to look both work alone. Name something you see after each look so the scan has a purpose.

What are good soccer scanning drills?

Wall passes with a shoulder check, a dribble where you look up on a beep, calling a friend's fingers before your first touch, and turning away from a friend who changes sides. Each makes you look and then use what you saw.

Is scanning the same as checking your shoulder?

Mostly, yes. Checking your shoulder is one type of scan. Scanning also includes looks forward and to the sides before the ball comes to you.

How many times per second does Xavi scan?

In Geir Jordet's research, as reported by Sky Sports, Xavi averaged 0.83 scans per second before receiving the ball, Frank Lampard 0.62 and Steven Gerrard 0.61. Jordet described anything over 0.50 as very high.

Is it too late to learn scanning at 16 or older?

No. Scanning is a habit of moving your head and knowing what to look for, and you can start training it at any age. Short sessions a few times a week and a clear match goal, like one look before every touch, are what build it.

Which positions scan the most?

In the Premier League study by Jordet and colleagues, central midfielders and central defenders scanned most often and forwards least. Every position benefits from it.
