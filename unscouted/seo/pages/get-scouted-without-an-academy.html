How do you get scouted for soccer, step by step?

You get scouted by playing where scouts already watch, performing the same way every time they come, and making yourself easy to check: a short video, a one-page CV and your next fixtures. Nobody gets scouted by waiting to be found. These seven steps work with or without an academy.

  1. Play at the highest level you can hold a place in. Scouts watch leagues, not parks. A regular starter one level up is more visible than a star one level down.
  2. Get better at what scouts judge. Decisions, off-ball movement, consistency and attitude, not tricks. Train the reading part with our free soccer IQ test and scanning test.
  3. Get filmed. One full match every few weeks. Cut a 3 to 5 minute highlight video and keep one full half as proof.
  4. Make a one-page football CV. Position, team, level, video links, next fixtures and a coach who can vouch for you.
  5. Go where doors are open. Open trials at non-league clubs in the UK, college ID camps and club tryouts in the US. See our guide to pro soccer tryouts.
  6. Contact coaches yourself. A short, specific email to ten clubs at your level beats a hundred to famous ones.
  7. Avoid anyone who charges for "guaranteed" scouts. Real clubs don't charge for trials, and real agents don't take fees up front.

Can you get scouted without an academy?

Most professional players came through academies. If you're 16 and not in one, the door is narrower. That's the truth, and any page that tells you otherwise is selling something.

The numbers in the US show how tight it is even one level below pro. According to the NCAA's own figures for 2024-25, about 5.9% of high school boys who play soccer go on to play NCAA college soccer, and only about 1.4% play at Division I.

But "narrow" is not "closed". Late routes exist and people do use them. Jamie Vardy is the famous English example: released by Sheffield Wednesday at 16, he went back to non-league football with Stocksbridge Park Steels, later moved to Fleetwood Town, and Leicester City paid £1 million for him before he won the Premier League in 2016. He's an outlier, not a plan. The useful lesson is that he kept playing at a level where he could be watched, and kept getting better.

Set your goal honestly. "Get scouted" is not something you can do. "Become the best player in my league, get into a higher-level team, and give coaches a reason to look twice" is. Good outcomes along the way include a better club, a semi-pro contract, a college place or simply being a much better player. All of those count.

What do soccer scouts look for?

You can't control your height, your birthday or who turns up to your game. You can control a lot of what scouts say they look for.

Gwynn Williams, a long-time scout who worked at Chelsea, told FourFourTwo it's about "the two a's, ability and attitude, you have to have both", and that he judges ability partly by decisions, such as whether a player sees the forward pass. A small University of Chichester study that interviewed five Premier League and Championship scouts also picked out decision making and risk-taking as key. So focus on four things:

  • Decisions. Do you pick the right option, quickly? Do you see the forward pass, or always play safe? This is the part most solo training ignores. Our decision-making guide shows how to train it alone.
  • Off-ball movement. What you do in the 85 minutes you don't have the ball. Do you move to make yourself an option? Do you get back into shape?
  • Consistency. One great game is luck. A scout who watches you twice and sees the same level both times has something to report.
  • Attitude. Body language after a mistake, how you talk to teammates, whether you work when the game is going badly. Scouts notice this from the touchline before you touch the ball.

Each position is judged on different decisions. Our guides for the striker, winger, number 10 and center back break down what scouts look for in each role. Skills and tricks matter, but they're the easiest thing to show and the least likely to separate you from everyone else at a trial.

Scouts usually describe what they judge in five areas. Here's what each means, and how much of it is in your hands:

AreaWhat scouts watchCan you train it alone?
TechnicalFirst touch, passing range, using both feet under pressureYes, with a ball and a wall
Game intelligenceScanning, decisions, seeing the forward pass, positioningYes, with match situations, film and self-review
PhysicalSpeed, endurance, strength in duels, how you movePartly: fitness yes, height and growth no
Mental and attitudeReaction to mistakes, effort when losing, coachabilityYes, and it's visible from the touchline
Potential and impactHow you change games now, and how much better you could getYes, by improving every season and playing up a level

Notice that scouts judge you in context: your age, your league, your role in the team. A strong game in a strong league tells them more than a great game in a weak one, which is why where you play matters so much.

How do scouts find players?

Most players are found in competitive matches, through recommendations, or at trials. Scouts watch leagues and tournaments where good players already are, follow up tips from coaches they trust, and increasingly check video before travelling. Very few players are found by sending cold messages on social media.

Where do scouts watch players?

Scouts go where good players already are. Your job is to get yourself into those places.

In the UK: non-league and the highest level you can reach

Below the four professional divisions, the National League System runs down through six steps of non-league football, with the National League at Step 1. Non-league is where late developers are most often found, because clubs higher up watch it and because the football is competitive with adults.

  • Look for pre-season open trials at local non-league clubs. Check the club's own website or official social accounts, not a third party.
  • Ask the manager directly about training with the reserves or the under-18s or under-23s side if the club has one. Short, polite message, your position, your current team, a link to your video.
  • Play up a level if you can hold your place. A regular starter at a higher step is more visible than a star at a lower one.

Big professional academies are harder to walk into. Chelsea, for example, says on its website that its academy for male players does not hold open trials. That's normal for top clubs, so put your energy where doors are open.

In the US: college soccer and ID camps

College soccer is the main late route. Besides NCAA Divisions I, II and III, there are NAIA and junior college programmes, so the range of levels is wide.

  • ID camps are camps run by college programmes where coaches see players in person. Pick camps at schools where you'd realistically fit, not just the famous ones.
  • Know the contact rules. SoccerWire's summary of NCAA rules says Division I and II coaches can't make recruiting contact before June 15 after your sophomore year. Rules change, so check the current NCAA calendar.
  • Email coaches yourself. Position, grad year, team, grades, a short video link and your next games. Coaches recruit players who make it easy.

What if there's no good club near you?

Then the nearest higher level is your target, even if it means travel for training twice a week. If that isn't possible yet, play the most competitive adult football you can (a strong amateur or semi-pro side often beats a weak youth team), film it, and use camps and open trials a few times a year. Don't move cities on the strength of a promise from someone you met online. If you're under 18, any move or trip is a family decision.

Do paid camps and showcases get you scouted?

Sometimes, but paying doesn't buy attention. A camp or showcase is only worth it if the coaches you want to impress are actually there. Before paying, ask which clubs or colleges will be watching, check that with them directly, and prefer events run by the club or college itself. College ID camps in the US are a normal part of recruiting; a "pro trial package" sold online usually isn't.

What should be in a highlight video for scouts?

Most highlight videos are a run of skills and goals. A coach or scout learns more from a shorter video that shows how you think.

  • Keep it short: 3 to 5 minutes. Best clips first.
  • Make yourself easy to find: freeze the first frame and circle or arrow yourself.
  • Show decisions, not just finishes: a scan then a forward pass, a run that pulls a defender away, a quick switch of play.
  • Show off-ball and defensive work: pressing, recovery runs, winning the ball back. These are rare in highlight videos, so they stand out.
  • Add one full half as a separate link. It proves the highlights weren't five lucky moments.
  • Put your details on the first screen: name, position, age or grad year, team, contact.

Should you use social media to get scouted?

Social media is a place to host your video and show you're serious, not a scouting system. Keep one clean account with your position, team and an email in the bio, post real match clips rather than only skills, and keep it free of anything you wouldn't want a coach to see. Never treat a DM as a trial invitation: real clubs use official channels.

How do you make a football CV?

A football CV (or player profile) is one page that lets a coach judge in 30 seconds whether to watch your video. It lists your details, positions, current team and level, a few honest facts and how to reach you. Keep it factual: coaches trust "regular starter, U18 regional league" more than "elite playmaker".

How do you contact clubs and coaches yourself?

Send a short, specific email to the right person: the first-team or youth manager at a non-league club, or the head or assistant coach at a college program. Say who you are, your position and level, why that club, and give your video, CV and next fixtures. One follow-up after about two weeks is fine; more than that is not.

Email template (edit every line for each club):

Subject: [Position], born [year], [current team and league] – video and next fixtures

Hi Coach [surname],

I'm [name], a [position] born in [year], playing for [team] in [league], where I've started [number] of [number] games this season. I'm interested in [club or college] because [one real reason: style of play, level, location].

Highlights (4 min): [link]. Full first half vs [opponent]: [link]. My one-page CV is attached.

My next games are [date, time, place] and [date, time, place]. My coach, [name], is happy to be contacted at [email].

Thank you for your time,
[Name] · [phone/email] · [parent's email if under 18]

Also ask your own coach to help. A call or email from a coach who has watched you every week counts for more than anything you write about yourself. And be realistic about where you send it: ten emails to clubs and colleges at your level beat a hundred to famous ones.

How do you spot scouting scams and fake agents?

Players who are desperate to be seen are a target. Learn the warning signs before someone messages you.

  • "Pay to trial with a pro club." Chelsea warns that fraudsters impersonate club staff to get money for trials and says the club "will never ask for money in connection with anything that refers to a trials process". Treat any message like that as a scam until the club itself confirms it.
  • Contact through social media or personal email. Chelsea also says it never invites young players to trials through social media or staff personal email addresses. Check by contacting the club through its official site.
  • An "agent" who wants a fee up front. A real agent earns money when a deal happens. Under FIFA's agent rules (as summarised by the Scottish FA), a licensed agent can't approach a minor or their parents earlier than six months before the age they can sign a first professional contract, and needs written consent from a parent or guardian first. An adult who approaches a 15-year-old with an offer and a fee is a warning sign.
  • Guaranteed contracts or guaranteed scouts. No one can guarantee either. Showcase events that charge a fee aren't automatically scams, but paying doesn't buy you attention. Ask who exactly will be watching and check it.

If you're under 18, involve a parent or guardian before you send money, documents or travel anywhere for a trial.

Do you need an agent to get scouted?

No. You don't need an agent to get noticed, and a real agent earns money when a deal happens, not by charging you up front. If you're under 18, FIFA's rules also limit when agents can approach you and require a parent or guardian to sign any agreement. FIFA rules restrict international transfers of players under 18 too, with narrow exceptions, so be very wary of paid "trial abroad" packages.

How do you scout yourself honestly?

After a match, rate yourself 1 to 5 on each line. Then write one piece of evidence from the game. No evidence means no score. Be harsher than you want to be: a scout will be.

AreaWhat to ask yourselfScore (1–5)Evidence from the match
ScanningDid I check around me before the ball arrived, most of the time?
Decisions on the ballDid I pick the best option, or the safest? Did I see the forward pass?
First touchDid my first touch move the ball where I wanted to go next?
Off-ball movementWas I an option for the player on the ball? Did I make runs that helped others?
Defending and transitionWhat did I do in the 3 seconds after we lost the ball?
ConsistencyWas my second half as good as my first? Did I disappear for long spells?
AttitudeBody language after mistakes. Talking to teammates. Effort when losing.

Do this after every match for 12 weeks and you'll have something most players at a trial don't: a clear idea of your own level and proof you've improved.

FAQ

Can you go pro without being in an academy?

Some players do, but it's rare and nobody can promise it. Late routes include non-league football in the UK and college soccer in the US. Focus on reaching the highest level you can and getting better at decisions, movement and consistency.

Is 16, 17 or 18 too late to get scouted?

It's later than the academy path, but not too late for non-league, semi-pro or college football. Jamie Vardy was released at 16 and rebuilt his career in non-league. Use the time to improve and to play at a level where people watch.

Do I need an agent?

Not to get noticed. Under FIFA's agent rules, licensed agents can't approach minors until six months before they can sign a first professional contract, and need a guardian's written consent. Be careful with anyone who asks for an upfront fee.

Are paid football trials worth it?

Treat any paid trial that claims to be with a professional club as a likely scam until the club confirms it. Chelsea, for example, says it never asks for money for trials. Paid showcases are not always scams, but ask who will be watching and verify it.

What do football scouts look for?

Scouts talk about ability and attitude. Ability includes decision making, such as seeing the forward pass, plus movement and consistency. Attitude includes body language, effort and how you treat teammates.

How do you get scouted for soccer in high school?

Play for the strongest club team you can, not only your high school team, because college coaches mostly recruit at club events and ID camps. Build a short highlight video, email coaches at colleges where you'd realistically fit, and check the current NCAA calendar for when coaches can contact you.

How do you get scouted as a goalkeeper?

The same way as outfield players, with one difference: your video must show decisions, not only saves. Include crosses you claimed, distribution, starting position, communication and how you deal with through balls. Coaches want to see a full half, because goalkeepers are judged on the quiet moments too.

Do you need to pay to get scouted?

No. Professional clubs don't charge for trials, and real agents don't charge up front. US club tryouts and college ID camps can have registration fees on the club's or college's official site, which is normal. Be wary of anyone selling guaranteed scouts or trials.

How do you get noticed by scouts without a club connection?

Make it easy to find and judge you: play at the highest level you can, keep a short video and a one-page CV ready, contact coaches directly with your next fixtures, and ask your current coach to recommend you. A recommendation from someone who watches you every week is the best connection you can have.

What can parents do to help a player get scouted?

Help find the right level of football and genuine trials, film matches, check that any trial or agent is real before paying or travelling, and handle contact for players under 18. Parents can't make scouts watch, but they can stop a player wasting time and money on scams.
