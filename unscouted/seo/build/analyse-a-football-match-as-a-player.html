How do you analyse a football match, step by step?

Analysing a football match (or analyzing a soccer game, in US spelling) takes six steps: choose one player in your position, set one question, track five things while you watch, take timed notes, compare with your own last match, and turn the biggest gap into one training target. The whole loop takes about an hour a week.

  1. Pick one player who plays your role in a system close to your team's.
  2. Write one question about something you struggle with, like "how does he get free from a tight marker?"
  3. Track five things for that player: scans, body shape, first touch, off-ball runs and the 3 seconds after losing the ball.
  4. Take timed notes: minute, what happened, what you noticed. Pause before he receives and predict.
  5. Compare with your own match on the same five things, using video if you have it.
  6. Set one countable target for the week, test it in training and in your next game.

What's the difference between watching as a fan and analysing as a player?

When you watch as a fan, your eyes follow the ball. You see goals, shots and fouls. That's fine for enjoying the game, but it teaches you almost nothing about your own job on the pitch.

Most guides on how to analyse (or analyze, if you're in the US and call it soccer) a match are written for analysts and coaches. They talk about formations, pressing triggers and expected goals. Useful for a coach. Too wide for a player.

As a player, you're studying one job: yours. A centre-back watching a centre-back, a winger watching a winger. Most of what that player does happens without the ball, which is exactly the part a fan never sees and a TV camera often cuts away from.

The rule: for the whole time you're analysing, the ball is not your subject. If you catch yourself watching the ball, find your player again.

What should you do before kick-off?

Two decisions before kick-off make the whole thing work.

1. Pick one player in your position

Choose someone who plays your role in a system close to your team's. If you're a full-back in a back four, watch a full-back in a back four, not a wing-back in a back five. If you're not sure, pick the player whose job you'd most like to copy.

2. Pick one question

Write down one question you want answered. Good questions are specific to something you struggle with. For example:

  • "Where does a holding midfielder stand when the centre-backs have the ball?"
  • "What does a winger do when the full-back behind them has the ball?"
  • "How does a striker get free from a centre-back who's marking tight?"

One question stops you trying to see everything and seeing nothing.

Practical tip: use full-match replays or extended highlights if you can, because normal highlights only show the ball. If you only have a TV broadcast, accept that your player will sometimes be off-screen and note what you can.

What should you track during the match?

You don't need to track all five every time. Start with two, add more as it gets easier. Use tally marks: one line each time you see it.

1. Scans before receiving

Does your player look over their shoulder before the ball reaches them? Count how many times they turn their head in the few seconds before a pass arrives. There is research behind this one. A 2020 study of one Premier League club's players across a season (Jordet and colleagues, in Frontiers in Psychology) found that players who scanned more before receiving were slightly more likely to complete their pass. The authors say the effect was small and the study shows a link, not proof of cause, but it's a habit you can see and copy. Check your own with our free football scanning test, and see how to scan in soccer for drills.

2. Body shape when receiving

Is their body open, side-on to the pitch, so they can see forward and play forward? Or square to the passer with their back to where they want to go? Mark "open" or "closed" for each receive you can see.

3. First-touch direction

Where does the first touch take the ball: forward, sideways, backwards, or dead at their feet? A first touch that moves the ball away from pressure and towards the next action is often a decision made before the ball arrived.

4. Off-ball runs

When a teammate has the ball, what does your player do? Count runs and note the type: towards the ball to offer a pass, in behind, or a run that drags a defender away to free someone else. Also note when they stand still.

5. The 3 seconds after losing the ball

The moment their team loses possession, does your player press the ball, cover a space, or switch off? This is where effort and understanding show, and it's rarely in highlights.

How do you analyse the whole team, not just one player?

Split the match into phases and watch how each team behaves in each one. Coaches and analysts use the same idea: a team with the ball, a team without it, and the moments in between. Once you know what your player's team is trying to do in each phase, his decisions make much more sense.

How should you take notes while watching?

Write short, timed notes: the minute, what happened and what you noticed. Notes like "23' gets free by stepping away from the 10 just before the pass" are useful a week later. "Played well" is not. On replays, pause before your player receives, predict, then play.

  • Use the clock. Note the minute for every entry so you can find the moment again.
  • Watch twice if you can. First time for the whole team's shape, second time only for your player.
  • Work in 15-minute blocks. Stop, read your notes, write one line on what you've learned so far.
  • Keep tallies, not essays. A line each time you see one of the five things is enough.
  • Save the best moments. Write down the minute of two or three clips to show yourself or a coach.

How do you compare a pro's game with your own?

Watching a pro only helps if you hold it up against yourself. Within a day or two, look at your own last match. Video is best. If you don't have any, write down what you remember honestly, or ask a teammate or coach.

Fill in the same five rows for yourself. Then ask one question: where is the biggest gap?

Using the example above, a player might notice: "He checks his shoulder before almost every receive. I mostly look at the ball until it arrives, then I have to turn under pressure." That gap is your target.

Don't compare everything. You won't have a Premier League player's speed or strength this week. Compare the things you can change by deciding to: where you look, how you stand, which way you take your touch, when you move.

How do you turn your notes into a training target?

One target per week. Write it as something you can count.

  • Weak: "Scan more."
  • Better: "Check both shoulders before every pass I receive in training this week."
  • Best: "Check both shoulders before every pass I receive. A teammate counts how many I miss in the first 10 minutes of the session."

Then use it in the next match and mark it on the worksheet. Next week, watch the same player again or pick a new question. After a few weeks you'll have a list of habits copied from players in your position, each tested in your own games.

Which games should you watch first?

Start with games where your player's team has a clear style, because patterns are easier to spot. A few starting points by position, with what to look for. These are things to check for yourself, not claims about any one match:

  • Holding midfielder: a deep midfielder in a possession team, such as Rodri. Watch where he stands when his centre-backs have the ball, and how often he looks around before it arrives.
  • Striker: a striker who links play as well as scores, such as Harry Kane. Watch when he drops towards the ball and when he stays high, and what happens to the space he leaves.
  • Winger: a winger who beats players one-on-one. Watch the moments before the 1v1: where they receive, which foot, and which way their body faces.
  • Centre-back: a centre-back in a team that plays out from the back. Watch body shape when receiving from the goalkeeper and where the first pass goes.

FAQ

How do footballers analyse games?

Players usually focus on their own role rather than the whole game. Pick one player in your position, track a few specific things like scanning, body shape and off-ball runs, then compare with your own match and choose one thing to work on.

What should I look for when watching football to improve?

Watch what one player in your position does without the ball: scans before receiving, body shape, where the first touch goes, the runs they make, and what they do in the 3 seconds after their team loses the ball.

How can I analyse my own football performance?

Film a match if you can, or ask a teammate or coach to watch. Rate yourself on the same five things you track for pros, write down evidence for each, find your biggest gap and turn it into one countable training target for the week.

Which players should I watch for my position?

Pick a player who plays your role in a system close to your team's. A full-back in a back four should watch a full-back in a back four. Teams with a clear style make patterns easier to spot.

Does watching football make you a better player?

Watching as a fan helps a little. Watching with a plan helps more: one player, one question, notes, then testing what you learned in your own training and matches.

How do you analyse a football match tactically?

Split it into phases: shape and formation, build-up, chance creation, defending, the moments after losing and winning the ball, and set pieces. Use the first 10 minutes to find both teams' shapes, then watch how each team behaves in each phase.

What questions should you ask when analysing a football match?

Ask about moments, not results. In possession: where does the player stand, how often does he look before receiving, where does his first touch go, when does he move. Out of possession: who does he press, what triggers it, what does he do in the 3 seconds after losing the ball.

How long does it take to analyse a football match?

For one player, watching one half with notes takes about an hour including pauses. Add 20 minutes to compare with your own match and set a target. Analysing a full match for both teams takes much longer, which is why players should start with one player and one question.

How do you take notes when analysing a football match?

Write short timed notes: the minute, what happened and what you noticed. Keep tallies for repeated actions, work in 15-minute blocks, and save the minutes of two or three key moments so you can watch them again.
