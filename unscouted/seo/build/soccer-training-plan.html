What is an individual soccer training plan?

An individual soccer training plan is a written schedule of what you practise on your own each week: which days, what you work on, and for how many minutes. It sits alongside your team's training, filling the gaps a team session can't, like extra touches, your weak foot, decision making and reviewing your own games.

Search for "soccer training plan" and most results are practice plans for coaches: how to run a 90-minute team session with warm-up, main part and game. That is useful if you coach. If you are a player aged 15–21 without an academy behind you, you need something different: a plan you can do with a ball, a wall and sometimes one friend.

How much should you train on your own?

For most players with 2–3 team sessions and a match each week, 2–3 solo sessions of 20–45 minutes is enough. More is not always better: your solo work should leave you fresh for team training and matches, and you need at least one full day off each week.

The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends one day of rest per week and two to three months off per year from any one sport, and says "off" means staying active in other ways, not sitting still. That is general guidance, not a personal prescription. If you have an injury, pain that doesn't go away, or a medical condition, talk to a doctor or physio before adding training.

  • Hard days: match day, team training days and one solo technique or fitness session.
  • Easy days: short technique work, game IQ, match review, light movement.
  • Rule of thumb: don't put two hard days back to back if you can avoid it.

How do you build a weekly soccer training plan?

Start by writing in your fixed days: team practice, match day and school. Then add a hard solo session in the middle of the week, light sessions next to the match, and one full rest day. Here is a typical week for a player with team practice on Tuesday and Thursday and a match on Saturday.

DayFocusMinutes (solo)
MondayRecovery: easy walk, jog or bike, stretching. Match review of Saturday's game.20–30
TuesdayTeam practice. Plus game IQ: 5 match situations before or after.10–15
WednesdayMain solo session: technique and fitness.40–45
ThursdayTeam practice. Nothing extra.0
FridayLight: short touches, scanning drill, game IQ for your position.15–20
SaturdayMatch.0
SundayFull rest.0

That is about 1.5–2 hours of solo work a week on top of team training. If your team practises three times, drop Friday's session or make it 10 minutes of game IQ only.

Game IQ sessions (10–20 minutes)

Work through match situations: look at the picture, decide what you would do and why, then check the answer. Do it before reading the options. Our free soccer IQ test has 8 situations to start with, and our page on decision-making training shows how to work through them. (In most of the world the game is called football, so you will see both words.)

Match analysis (20 minutes)

The day after a match, write down three moments: one good decision, one bad one, and one you aren't sure about. For each, note what you saw and what you missed. If you have video, check it. Our guide to analysing a match as a player goes deeper.

How do you progress a soccer training plan over 12 weeks?

Split 12 weeks into three 4-week blocks. Each block keeps the same weekly shape but makes the work a little harder or more game-like, and ends with an easier week and the same simple tests. Change one or two things per block, not everything at once, so you can tell what is working.

WeeksBlockWhat changesEnd-of-block check
1–4HabitLearn the routine. Clean technique, scan before every touch, review every match.Wall-pass count, situation score, sprint time.
5–8PressureFewer touches (two-touch, then one-touch), more weak foot, shorter time to decide in game IQ work.Same tests. Compare with week 4.
9–12GamePosition-specific work, more realistic drills with a friend, focus on the mistake your reviews keep showing.Same tests, plus compare your match reviews from weeks 1 and 12.

Add load slowly. A simple rule: don't raise more than one thing at a time (reps, speed or difficulty) from one week to the next. If you feel run-down, sore for days, or your sleep gets worse, go back a step and take an extra easy day.

How do you fit a training plan around team practice and school?

Treat team practice and matches as fixed, then fit short solo sessions around them. Keep your hardest solo session at least two days from a match, use 10–15 minute game IQ sessions on busy days, and cut back to the minimum in exam weeks instead of dropping everything.

  • Mornings work: 15 minutes of touches before school is easier to keep than 45 minutes late at night.
  • Game IQ needs no pitch: situations and match review fit into a bus ride or a study break.
  • Talk to your coach: if your team already does heavy fitness, skip yours that week.

How do you track progress in your training plan?

Keep a simple log of what you did each session, and repeat the same few tests every four weeks under the same conditions. Progress in soccer is slow and uneven, so write things down: your memory will either overrate or underrate how far you have come.

Hand timing on a phone isn't precise, so only compare your own times under the same conditions. The most useful tracker is usually the match review: when the same mistake stops showing up, that is real progress.

Honest note: a plan won't make you a pro, and no plan works the same for everyone. What it does is make your extra work consistent and pointed at your real weaknesses. Adjust it when your reviews tell you to.

FAQ

How do I make my own soccer training plan?

Write in your team practices, matches and school first. Add one main solo session mid-week, short sessions next to match day, a recovery and review day after the match, and one full rest day. Then decide what each session trains: technique, game IQ, fitness, recovery or match analysis.

What should an individual soccer training plan include?

Technique (touches, passing, weak foot), game IQ (decisions and scanning), fitness (short sprints and change of direction), recovery (rest, sleep, light movement) and analysis of your own matches. Most solo plans only cover technique, which leaves the rest to chance.

How many days a week should a soccer player train alone?

For most players with 2–3 team sessions, 2–3 short solo sessions is enough. Keep at least one full rest day; the American Academy of Pediatrics recommends one day of rest per week for young athletes.

Can I train soccer every day?

Daily short touches or game IQ work are fine for many players, but hard training every day isn't a good idea. Keep one full rest day a week and listen to soreness and tiredness.

What should I do the day before a game?

Keep it light: 15–20 minutes of easy touches, a scanning drill and a few situations for your position. Save sprints and hard sessions for mid-week.
