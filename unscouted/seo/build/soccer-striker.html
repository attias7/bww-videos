What is a striker in soccer?

A striker is the forward who plays in the middle of the attack, nearest to the other team's goal, and whose first job is scoring. Strikers usually stand on or near the opponent's last defender, so they are the player the team looks for when it wants to go forward quickly or finish a move.

In football (what most of the world calls soccer), the position is also called center forward or "the 9", because in the old system of shirts 1 to 11 by position the center forward wore 9.

Striker and center forward are used interchangeably today. The fine difference: "center forward" describes where you stand (the middle of the front line), while "striker" describes what you do (finish chances). A false 9, for example, is a center forward who doesn't behave like a classic striker.

What does a striker do in a game?

A striker scores goals, makes runs that stretch the defense, holds the ball up so teammates can join the attack, and starts the team's press when the other team has the ball. The balance between these jobs depends on the formation and on the type of striker the coach wants.

  • Score from crosses, through balls, cut-backs and rebounds.
  • Stretch the defense. Runs in behind push their line deeper and open space for your midfielders.
  • Hold up and link. Protect the ball with your back to goal and lay it off.
  • Press first. You decide which way their build-up goes.
  • Attack the box. Pick the right spot for crosses and follow every shot.

What skills does a striker need?

A striker needs accurate finishing with both feet, a first touch that sets up the shot, smart movement off the ball, the strength to hold the ball up, timing in the air, and the discipline to press. Finishing and movement matter most.

How does a striker decide where to run and when to shoot?

A striker decides by reading three things before the ball arrives: whether the passer has time, where the space is behind or beside the defenders, and where the keeper and nearest defender stand. Most goals are decided by the run chosen a second or two before the pass.

Movement before the ball

Time your run off the passer. If he is facing forward with nobody close, he can play the ball in behind, so go. If he is under pressure or facing his own goal, come short and help him. A small step towards the ball first drags the center back with you.

Playing on the shoulder (and the offside line)

"On the shoulder" means standing level with the last defender, on his blind side, ready to spin in behind. Under Law 11 of the official Laws of the Game, you are onside if you are level with the second-last opponent (usually the last outfield defender), and you can't be offside in your own half or from a throw-in, goal kick or corner. Your head, body and feet count; your arms don't. Curve your run along the line so you stay onside until the pass is played.

Near post or far post?

Before a cross, count the bodies. Near post empty: attack it early. Keeper and defenders crowding the near post: go far post. Winger on the byline and defenders dropping onto the goal line: stop at the penalty spot for the cut-back.

Shoot or pass?

  • Shoot if you can hit the target and no defender is in the shooting lane.
  • Pass if a teammate has a clearly better chance and the passing lane is open.
  • If the defender sits in the passing lane, the shot is usually on. If he blocks the shot, the pass usually is.
  • Don't take an extra touch to "get closer" with a defender recovering behind you.

Pressing triggers

Curve your run to cut off the pass into the middle, then sprint on a trigger: a back pass, a bad first touch, a center back receiving facing his own goal, a pass out to the full-back near the touchline, or the keeper receiving on his weaker foot. Three striker situations on a tactics board

Look at each board, pick A, B or C, then check the pro pick. You are the lime player; their goal is at the top.

Situation 1: your midfielder has the ball, you're on their back line

It is 0-0 after 15 minutes. Your central midfielder has the ball just inside their half. You're on their back line.

What do you do?

  • A Come short towards him for the ball.
  • B Stay between the center backs and call for it.
  • C Curve across the left center back and run in behind. (pro pick)

Why C: The passer faces forward with nobody near him, and there are about 20 meters of empty grass behind their line. Curve your run so the center back loses sight of you. Why not A or B: coming short is for when the passer is under pressure, and standing still is easy to mark.

Situation 2: a cross is coming from the left

It is 1-1 after 60 minutes. Your left winger is about to cross from the left. You're arriving in the box.

Where do you run?

  • A Attack the near post.
  • B Run to the far post. (pro pick)
  • C Hold your spot near the penalty spot.

Why B: Their right back is still near the edge of the box, more than 15 meters from the far post, so nobody guards it. Why not A or C: the keeper and a center back already fill the near post, and the penalty spot suits a low cut-back from the byline, not a cross from this deep.

Situation 3: shoot or square it?

It is 0-0 after 78 minutes. Counter attack. You've carried it into the right side of the box. A teammate is running with you on the left.

What do you do?

  • A Shoot low across the keeper into the far corner. (pro pick)
  • B Square it to your teammate.
  • C Take one more touch to get closer.

Why A: Their center back has stepped into the passing lane, not the shooting lane, and the keeper is guarding his near post. Shoot low, now. Why not B or C: the square pass runs straight into the center back, and an extra touch lets the recovering defender catch you.

In all three, the answer came from one detail you could only see by looking early. That's why strikers need to scan too, even though forwards scanned the least in a well-known study of Premier League players.

How do you study a striker on TV?

Watch the striker, not the ball, for 10 minutes at a time. Pick one striker whose type fits yours and note what he does before the ball reaches him. Highlights never show that part.

  • Runs: how many in behind, how many short, how many across a defender?
  • Crosses: where does he stand before the cross, and when does he move?
  • First touch: does it go away from the defender, or set up a shot?
  • Shots: how many first time, how many low, how many on target?
  • Pressing: who does he press, from which side, and what makes him sprint?

What are the most common striker mistakes?

The most common striker mistakes are shooting for power instead of the target, standing still, running offside, pointing at space without running, and stopping after a teammate shoots.

  • Blasting it. A hard shot over the bar is worth nothing. Low and on target first.
  • Standing still. Keep making runs, even when the ball doesn't come.
  • Pointing, not running. A run you only point at doesn't exist for the passer. If you see it, go.
  • Drifting offside. Get back level early and quietly, then go again.
  • Not following shots. Every time a teammate shoots, run at the goal for the rebound.
  • Forcing it with your back to goal. If you can't turn, lay it off or win the foul.

FAQ

What is a striker in soccer?

A striker is the forward who plays closest to the opponent's goal, in the middle of the attack. Their main job is scoring, and they also run in behind, hold the ball up and press first.

What number does a striker wear?

Traditionally number 9, from the old system where shirts 1 to 11 matched positions. Second strikers often wear 10, and with squad numbers today a striker can wear any number.

What is the difference between a striker and a center forward?

Today they mean almost the same thing and are used interchangeably. "Center forward" describes where you play, the middle of the front line, while "striker" describes the job of finishing chances.

What does a striker do when the team doesn't have the ball?

The striker presses first. He curves his run to cut off the pass into the middle, pushes their build-up to one side, and sprints on triggers like a back pass or a bad touch.

What is a false 9?

A false 9 starts as the striker but drops into midfield to get the ball. That pulls center backs out of position and opens space for wingers and midfielders to run into. Lionel Messi played the role under Pep Guardiola at Barcelona.

Do you have to be tall or fast to be a striker?

No. Height helps a target man and pace helps a runner, but poachers and false 9s win with timing, movement and first touch. Pick the type of striker that fits what you already do well.

How can I become a better striker on my own?

Do two finishing sessions a week where only shots on target count, one movement and speed session, and study a striker on TV. Practise decisions too, for example with Unscouted's free soccer IQ test.
