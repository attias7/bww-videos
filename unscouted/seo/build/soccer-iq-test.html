How does the soccer IQ test work?

You pick your position, then play 8 match situations built for that role. Each one is a tactics board with the minute and the score, a short line of text and 3 options. You have 15 seconds to choose. After each answer you see the pro pick as an arrow and a plain-English reason why.

RuleHow it works
PositionsStriker, winger, No. 10, midfielder or defender
Situations8 per position, each a real match moment on a tactics board. Or pick "All positions" to play all 40
Options3 per situation (A, B, C)
Timer15 seconds. If it runs out, the situation counts as missed
Scoring1 point for the pro pick only. "Close" options are flagged but score 0
ResultScore out of 8 (or 40), a level, a skill-by-skill breakdown and a score card you can share
Time neededAbout 3 minutes for 8, about 12 minutes for all 40
CostFree, no sign-up (checked October 2026)

The timer is there for a reason. In a game the defender would already be on you, so a slow right answer isn't really a right answer. 15 seconds is still far more than you get on the pitch, but it stops you studying the board for a minute.

Scoring is strict on purpose. Some wrong options are "close": a decent choice, just not the best one for that exact picture. The test tells you when you picked one of those, so you know you were thinking along the right lines, but it still scores zero. If you picked something else, the explanation also says why your option was weaker in that moment.

What is soccer IQ?

Soccer IQ, or football IQ, is how well you read a game and make decisions in it: seeing where the space and the free players are before the ball arrives, knowing what the score and the minute ask for, and picking the best option quickly. It's about what you do on the pitch, not what you know about football.

Coaches often call it game intelligence or game sense. It shows up in every phase of play:

PhaseWhat a high soccer IQ looks likeTypical low-IQ habit
With the ballKnows the next pass before receiving, plays forward when it's on, keeps it when it isn'tReceives, then looks up, then decides
Without the ball (attacking)Moves to where the ball can actually reach, times runs with the passer's head upStands in a passing lane that's already blocked
DefendingReads the run that matters, keeps the line, delays instead of diving inFollows the ball, gets pulled out of position
TransitionsReacts in the first seconds after the ball changes handsStops to complain or jogs back
Game statePlays differently at 1-0 up in the 88th minute than at 0-0 in the 20thPlays every minute the same way

None of this is general intelligence. A player who struggles in a classroom can read a game brilliantly, and the other way round. Soccer IQ is pattern recognition built from seeing the same kinds of moments many times.

How do you read your soccer IQ score?

Your score out of 8 gives you a level, but the skill breakdown is the useful part. It shows which type of decision cost you points. A total of 6 with both misses on "scanning before the ball" tells you exactly what to train, which a single number never can.

ScoreLevelWhat it usually meansWhat to do next
8/8AnalystYou read every situation like a coach.Do it at full speed in games. Take another position's test to stretch yourself.
6–7PlayerGood instincts with a few gaps.Find the skill you missed and watch for that exact moment in games.
5 or lessFanYou're probably watching the ball more than the game.Very common and very trainable. Start with the 4-week plan below.

Be honest with yourself: 8 situations is a snapshot, not a verdict. One guess or one misread board can move you a whole level. Football also rarely has one perfect answer; the pro pick is the best choice for that exact picture, score and minute. Use your result to find what to work on, not to decide how good you are.

Write down the skill that cost you most. Retake the test in a few weeks and see whether the same skill keeps coming up. If it does, that's not bad luck; it's a habit.

Example situation, explained

Here's one situation that isn't in the test, so you can see how a question works and how the explanation reads. You're a center back. Try to decide before you read the options.

  • A Follow him and get tight before he turns
  • B Stay in the back line, shift across for the run, and let your midfielder press him (pro pick)
  • C Drop deep towards your own box

Why B is the pro pick: look at their winger. He's running inside, straight into your channel, and your left back is too far up the pitch to track him. Their striker is facing his own goal, and your defensive midfielder is right next to him. So your midfielder presses the striker, and you shift across and pick up the run.

Why not A: following a striker who drops is often right: you stop him turning. But only when your cover is set. Here, the gap you'd leave is exactly where the winger is running.

Why not C: dropping deep gives their striker a free turn and lets him run at your back line. You only need to cover one run, not pull the whole line back.

The cue was the winger's run and your left back's position, not the striker. In most situations, the player on the ball isn't the detail that decides it.

How do you get an honest result?

Take the test cold, alone and in one go, without pausing or re-reading the board after the timer starts. That's the only way the score reflects how you actually read a game. Taking it twice in a row mostly tests your memory of the answers, not your soccer IQ.

  • First run counts. Treat the first attempt for each position as your baseline. Screenshot or write down the score and the skill breakdown.
  • Decide, then read the explanation. Before reading why the pro pick is right, say out loud what cue you used. If your cue was the player on the ball, that's the habit to change.
  • Wait 3 to 4 weeks before retaking it. Use that time to train the skill you missed, then compare.
  • Try a second position. Many players have a strong position and a weak one. The No. 10 and midfielder tests overlap the most.

How can you improve your soccer IQ after the test?

You improve soccer IQ by seeing more game moments and deciding on them on purpose. On your own, that means watching one player with pauses, scanning in every session, and reviewing your own decisions. Four weeks of focus on the skill you missed is a sensible first block before you retake the test. To fit it around your team sessions, use our soccer training plan.

WeekFocusWhat to do (about 20 minutes, 3 times a week)
1See the pictureWatch 15 minutes of a pro in your position. Pause before each time he gets the ball, say what you'd do, then press play and compare.
2Your missed skillWatch only for moments of the skill that cost you points (for example, every time a back line steps up). Count them, note the cue each time.
3Take it to the pitchIn training and wall-pass sessions, check your shoulder before every touch. Name one thing you saw, every time.
4Review yourselfWatch a clip of your own game (or think back through it). Find 3 decisions, ask "what did I see, what was on, what did I pick?"

Three decision rules help in almost every situation in the test and on the pitch:

  1. Look past the ball. The detail that decides a moment is usually a run, a gap or a free player, not the ball carrier.
  2. Check the score and the minute before any risky pass or dribble. Game state changes the answer more often than players think.
  3. Forward if it's on, keep it if it isn't. The best option is the most dangerous one that's actually available, not the most dangerous one on paper.

Soccer IQ terms explained

Knowledge quizzes often ask about tactical terms. Knowing them helps you understand what coaches mean, and it helps you spot the cue faster in a situation test. Here are the terms that come up most, in plain English.

TermWhat it meansWhat it changes for you
PressingClosing down the player on the ball as a group to force a mistake or a long ballKnow who you press and which pass you block with your run
Counter-press (gegenpress)Pressing straight away after losing the ball, usually for a few seconds, to win it back high upThe moment you lose it, react forward instead of jogging back
Low blockA team defending deep and compact, close to its own box, leaving little space between the linesAgainst it, be patient: switch play and look for cut-backs
High line and offside trapDefenders stepping up together so attackers are caught offside when the pass is playedAs an attacker, time your run on the passer, not on the defender
OverloadHaving more players than the opponent in one area, like 3 v 2 on a wingSpot the side where your team has the extra player and play there
Switching playMoving the ball quickly from one side of the pitch to the otherWhen their block shifts toward the ball, the far side is usually free
Between the linesThe space between the opponent's midfield and defenceReceiving there lets you turn and attack the back line
False 9A centre forward who drops into midfield instead of staying highDefenders must decide whether to follow, which opens space behind
TransitionThe seconds when the ball changes team, from attack to defence or the other wayMost goals start here: decide fast whether to counter or recover
Rest defenceThe players a team keeps behind the ball while attacking, ready for a counterDefenders and holding midfielders: stay in position to stop the break

FAQ

Is a soccer IQ test the same as the Wonderlic test?

No. The Wonderlic is a general reasoning test, used for example at the NFL Combine. A soccer IQ test like this one shows you match situations and asks what you'd do, so it measures game reading and decisions, not general problem solving.

How many questions should a soccer IQ test have?

Enough to cover the decisions your position faces. This test uses 8 situations per position, so it takes about 3 minutes. For a fuller read, the all-positions mode runs all 40 situations in about 12 minutes. Knowledge quizzes often use 10 to 25 questions, but more trivia questions don't tell you more about how you read a game.

What is a good soccer IQ test score?

On this test, 8 out of 8 is the Analyst level, 6 or 7 is Player level and 5 or less is Fan level. On all 40 situations, 38 or more is Analyst level and 28 or more is Player level. The skill breakdown matters more than the total, because it shows which type of decision you keep missing.

Is a soccer IQ test accurate?

It's a snapshot. 8 situations is enough to show which skills you get right and which you miss, but one guess can change your level, which is why the 40-situation mode gives a steadier read. Use the result to find what to work on, and take it again later to see if the same gaps come up.

Is soccer IQ the same as your real IQ?

No. Soccer IQ is a nickname for game intelligence: reading situations and deciding well on the pitch. This test doesn't measure general intelligence, and a high or low result says nothing about how you'd do on an IQ test.

Can you improve your football IQ?

Yes. Decision making improves with practice, like any other part of your game. Watching pros in your position with pauses, scanning more in training and reviewing your own decisions all help. Our soccer IQ training guide has a full plan.

How long does it take to improve decision making?

There's no fixed timeline, and it depends on how much you train it. Training it on purpose every week helps more than waiting for it to come with games. Spotting the option on a screen comes first; making that decision in a real game, at speed, takes longer.
